# Boss Update Formatter

Turn `$ARGUMENTS` into a message ready to send to a boss, manager, or stakeholder — sounding like it was actually typed by a person, not generated by an AI.

`$ARGUMENTS` format: `<raw content to convert> | <optional context: recipient, channel, tone>`

Examples:
- `/boss-update We finished the migration but it took 2 extra days because the vendor API was undocumented. Team did great. | boss is direct, prefers Slack, keep it short`
- `/boss-update <paste of a long conversation/thread about a bug and how it got fixed>`

If no context is given after `|`, ask only what's essential: channel (email/Slack/WhatsApp/in person) and how formal the relationship is. Don't ask more than that — infer the rest from the content.

## Workflow

### 1. Understand What Actually Happened

Read the raw content and extract, before writing anything:
- What happened / what the news is (good, bad, or neutral)
- What decisions were made, and by whom
- What's blocked, what's needed, what the ask is (if any)
- Who deserves credit — specific names, not "the team"
- Any mistake, delay, or bad news that needs to be delivered

### 2. Apply Carnegie's Principles

Decide how each of these applies to this specific message. Skip whichever don't fit — don't force all of them in:

- **Don't criticize, condemn, or complain.** If a vendor, a teammate, or a past decision caused a problem, describe the situation and the fix — never assign blame in writing.
- **Give honest, specific appreciation.** Credit people by name for what they actually did. Skip generic praise ("great job team!") — it reads as filler.
- **Talk in terms of the other person's interests.** A boss cares about risk, timeline, cost, and what they need to tell their own boss — not the technical mechanism. Lead with that.
- **Arouse an eager want.** If you need something from them (approval, budget, a decision), frame it as *their* win, not your ask.
- **Let them feel ownership of the idea.** If the update follows a direction they gave, say so — tie it back to their call.
- **Admit mistakes quickly and plainly.** If something went wrong on your end, say it in one direct sentence. No hedging, no burying it in the third paragraph, no passive voice ("mistakes were made").
- **Begin in a friendly way, get to the point fast.** Bosses skim. One warm, human opening line, then the actual news.

### 3. Strip Every AI Tell

This step is the one most likely to get skipped — don't skip it. Rewrite until none of the following are present:

**Banned phrases/openers:**
- "I hope this message finds you well"
- "I wanted to reach out to..."
- "I'm excited to share..."
- "Furthermore" / "Moreover" / "Additionally" as sentence openers
- "In conclusion" / "To summarize" / "Overall"
- "It's important to note that..."
- "Let me know if you have any questions/concerns!"
- "Please don't hesitate to reach out"

**Structural tells:**
- Perfectly symmetric bullet lists for something that's really just two sentences of news
- Bold headers in a Slack DM or WhatsApp message (humans don't format chat messages like a report)
- Three-item lists everywhere ("robust, scalable, and efficient")
- Em-dash-heavy sentences used as a crutch for every transition
- An emoji on every line, or a summary emoji at the end (✅ 🎉 🚀) when the person doesn't actually text that way
- A closing line that restates everything already said above it

**Tone tells:**
- Uniform sentence length and rhythm (real people write a choppy sentence, then a longer one, then a fragment)
- Over-hedging ("It seems that possibly, in some cases...")
- Corporate filler words: "leverage," "seamless," "robust," "streamline," "circle back," "align," "synergy" — unless that's genuinely how this person or company talks
- Perfect grammar and zero contractions in a casual channel — use natural contractions if the channel is informal

### 4. Match the Real Channel and Relationship

- **Email to a formal boss**: short paragraphs, clear subject line, one ask per email, sign-off matching how they actually sign off (not "Best regards" if they never say that).
- **Slack/Teams/WhatsApp**: no headers, no bullet-heavy formatting, write like a text message — plain lines, maybe one line break, get to the point in the first sentence.
- **In-person talking points**: short phrases, not full sentences, no formatting at all.

If the user provides a sample of how they normally write (a past message, their usual tone), match its length, punctuation habits, and vocabulary instead of the defaults above.

### 5. Write the Message

Produce one finished message, in the same language as the original content (don't translate unless asked). Keep it as short as the news allows — a boss update is not an essay.

### 6. Review Before Showing the User

- [ ] Would a person actually type this, or does it read like a template?
- [ ] Does it lead with what the boss cares about, not the technical detail?
- [ ] Is credit given by name where it's due?
- [ ] If something went wrong, is it owned in one clear sentence, not buried or blamed elsewhere?
- [ ] Does the length and formatting match the channel (no report-style formatting in a chat message)?
- [ ] Did you check it against the banned phrases/tells list in step 3?

## Wrap Up

Show the user the finished message as plain text (not wrapped in headers or a big markdown block, unless it's a formal email) and ask:

> "Here's the message. Want it shorter, more direct, warmer, or a different tone?"

Offer to adjust length or formality — don't ask for anything else unless the recipient or channel was genuinely ambiguous.
